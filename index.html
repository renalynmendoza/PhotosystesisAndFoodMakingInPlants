01 — Overview

What is Photosynthesis?

Photosynthesis is the biological process by which green plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy — primarily from the sun — into chemical energy stored as glucose. It is one of the most fundamental reactions sustaining nearly all life on Earth.

The word itself comes from the Greek: photo meaning "light," and synthesis meaning "to put together." Plants put sunlight together with simple raw materials to manufacture their own food — a feat of molecular engineering no human technology has truly replicated.

02 — Ingredients

What Plants Need

Three essential raw materials feed the photosynthetic machine — each arriving from a completely different corner of the plant's environment.

Sunlight

Electromagnetic Radiation

Absorbed mainly in the red and blue wavelengths by chlorophyll pigments embedded in the thylakoid membranes. This radiant energy drives the entire photosynthetic engine, splitting water molecules and powering ATP synthesis.

Water

H₂O

Absorbed through the roots and transported upward via the xylem. Water molecules are split during the light-dependent reactions in a process called photolysis, releasing electrons and oxygen as a by-product.

Carbon Dioxide

CO₂

Diffuses into the leaf through microscopic pores called stomata, mostly on the underside of leaves. In the Calvin Cycle, carbon dioxide molecules are captured and "fixed" into organic carbon compounds — the building blocks of glucose.

03 — Mechanism

How It Works

Photosynthesis unfolds in two interconnected stages, both taking place inside the chloroplast — the green organelle found in plant cells.

Thylakoids
Stroma

Chloroplast — site of photosynthesis

01

Light-Dependent Reactions

Capturing the Sun

These reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. When sunlight strikes chlorophyll, it excites electrons to a higher energy state. These energized electrons travel down an electron transport chain, releasing energy that is used to produce ATP and NADPH — the cell's energy currency. Meanwhile, water molecules are split (photolysis), releasing oxygen into the air as a by-product — the very oxygen we breathe.

02

Calvin Cycle · Light-Independent

Building Glucose

Taking place in the fluid-filled stroma, the Calvin Cycle uses the ATP and NADPH from Stage 1 to convert carbon dioxide from the air into glucose. An enzyme called RuBisCO "fixes" carbon dioxide molecules, and through a series of chemical reactions, a 3-carbon molecule (G3P) is assembled. Multiple G3P molecules are then combined to form glucose — the plant's food.

03

Role of Chlorophyll

The Green Pigment

Chlorophyll is the green pigment inside chloroplasts that gives plants their color. It absorbs red and blue light most efficiently while reflecting green light — which is why we see plants as green. There are two main types: chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, working together to capture the broadest possible spectrum of usable sunlight.

04

Stomata & Gas Exchange

The Leaf's Breathing Pores

Tiny pores called stomata on leaf surfaces control gas exchange. CO₂ enters and O₂ exits through these openings, which are guarded by specialized cells that can open or close depending on light, temperature, and water availability. On a hot, dry day, stomata close to prevent water loss — temporarily slowing photosynthesis.

04 — Products

What Plants Produce

Two extraordinary outputs emerge from the photosynthetic process — one fuels all life, the other makes that life possible.

Glucose

C6H12O6

The primary energy-rich sugar produced by photosynthesis. Plants use glucose as fuel for growth, repair, and reproduction. It is stored as starch or converted into cellulose for structural support. When animals eat plants, they access this stored solar energy.

Oxygen

O2

Released as a by-product when water is split during the light reactions, oxygen diffuses out through the stomata into the atmosphere. Virtually all the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere was produced by photosynthetic organisms over billions of years of evolution.

05 — Significance

Why It Matters

Photosynthesis is not just a plant process — it is the cornerstone of life on Earth, with implications that ripple through every ecosystem, climate system, and food chain on the planet.

01

Foundation of All Food Chains

Plants are primary producers — the very first link in virtually every food chain. By converting sunlight into chemical energy, they make that energy available to herbivores, which in turn feed carnivores. Without photosynthesis, no food chain could exist.

02

Oxygen for Life

Every breath you take is made possible by photosynthesis. The 21% oxygen concentration in Earth's atmosphere is maintained almost entirely by photosynthetic organisms — from giant rainforest trees to microscopic marine phytoplankton.

03

Carbon Dioxide Regulation

Plants absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere during photosynthesis, making them critical carbon sinks. Forests and oceans sequester vast amounts of carbon, playing an essential role in moderating Earth's climate and counteracting greenhouse gas emissions.

04

Fossil Fuels — Ancient Sunlight

Coal, oil, and natural gas are the fossilized remains of ancient photosynthetic organisms. The energy locked in these fuels is, in essence, sunlight captured by plants hundreds of millions of years ago — making photosynthesis the original source of most energy humans use today.

05

Agriculture & Global Food Supply

All crops — wheat, rice, corn, vegetables, fruits — grow through photosynthesis. Understanding and optimizing this process is central to developing drought-resistant crops, improving yields, and feeding a growing global population in the face of climate change.
